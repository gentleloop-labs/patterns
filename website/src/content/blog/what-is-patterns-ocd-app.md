<p class="lead">
Patterns is a private OCD journal and tracker built around a simple observation: OCD often repeats a recognizable cycle even when the subject of a thought changes. The app gives you a place to note a trigger, the urge or compulsion that followed, and how you responded. It is meant to support reflection and ERP practice, not diagnose you or tell you what a thought means.
</p>

This is an article about an app we make, so the product descriptions below are ours. The explanation of OCD and [exposure and response prevention (ERP)](/erp) comes from independent clinical sources linked at the end. Those are different kinds of claims, and neither proves that using Patterns improves a person's symptoms.

## OCD has themes, but it also has a pattern

Someone might worry about whether the stove is off, whether a message was hurtful, or whether an intrusive thought says something about their character. Those concerns are not interchangeable, and none alone establishes OCD. What can repeat is the sequence underneath:

1. A thought, image, sensation, or situation triggers doubt.
2. Distress rises, often with an urgent need to resolve the doubt.
3. A person checks, seeks reassurance, avoids, or performs a mental ritual.
4. Relief arrives briefly, but the doubt comes back and invites the same response.

The [International OCD Foundation's OCD cycle resource](https://iocdf.org/resource/the-ocd-cycle/) describes this relationship between triggers, distress, compulsions or avoidance, and temporary relief. Our [plain-language OCD guide](/ocd) explains the broader picture, including why intrusive thoughts alone are not a diagnosis.

This is the connection behind the name *Patterns*. The app is not trying to catalogue every possible OCD theme. It is designed to make the recurring *response* easier to see. A concern about a lock and a concern about an awkward conversation may look unrelated, yet both could end in another check or another hour of [mental review](/blog/mental-review-ocd).

## What a Patterns entry is for

Suppose you notice an urge to reread a message for the fifth time. A brief entry might say: “Sent a message; worried it sounded rude; reread it four times; wanted to ask a friend; went back to my task instead.” That is a fictional example, not a template you need to follow.

The useful part is not proving whether the message was perfect. It is noticing the trigger, the response, and what happened next. Patterns lets you record OCD events, keep dated journal notes, and review trends over time. A distress rating can add context, but it is an estimate, not a score for how well you handled the moment. You can leave an entry short or skip a day entirely.

Across weeks, a record may help you notice that reassurance follows several different fears, or that one particular situation often leads to avoidance. It may also give you something more concrete to discuss with a therapist than “I had a difficult week.” The app cannot interpret that pattern for you or decide whether the event was OCD.

## Where ERP fits

ERP is an evidence-based psychological treatment for OCD. In therapy, a clinician helps a person identify relevant triggers and practice approaching them while reducing compulsive responses. The [International OCD Foundation's ERP guide](https://iocdf.org/about-ocd/ocd-treatment-guide/exposure-response-prevention/) describes assessment, collaborative planning, and practice between sessions. ERP is not simply trying to make anxiety vanish on demand.

Patterns includes a compulsion-delay tool and ways to record ERP practice because the difficult choice often happens outside an appointment: *Do I check again, ask for reassurance, review the memory, or leave the question open for now?* A tool can help you remember what you intended to practice. It cannot determine a safe or appropriate exposure for every situation, and it should not override a clinician's plan or ordinary safety needs.

The emphasis is on what you do next, not on suppressing thoughts. That is why the app is a companion to [ERP](/erp), not an automatic therapist. If you are considering professional care, our guide to [finding an OCD-informed therapist](/blog/how-to-find-an-ocd-therapist) covers questions you can ask about ERP experience.

## Private by design, without a perfection rule

Patterns keeps personal OCD records on your device and does not require an account. That matters when the subject of an entry is difficult to write down. You can read more about the product choices in [why Patterns keeps your OCD data private](/blog/why-patterns-stays-private).

Privacy alone does not make a journal helpful. Tracking can become another ritual if you repeatedly edit an entry until it feels “right,” compare distress numbers for certainty, or open the app to check whether you are recovering correctly. We discuss that risk openly in [when an OCD app becomes a compulsion](/blog/when-an-ocd-app-becomes-a-compulsion). The best use of Patterns may be a brief note, a chosen next step, and then closing the app to return to your day.

## What Patterns cannot tell you

Patterns cannot diagnose OCD, decide whether a specific thought is dangerous, measure a clinical outcome from one entry, or replace an assessment. It is also not a crisis service. A clinician can consider symptoms alongside a person's history, circumstances, and other possible explanations in a way an app cannot.

If the loop described here feels familiar, you do not have to identify the “correct” theme before asking for help. You can simply describe what keeps repeating and how much time it takes. If you want a private place to notice those responses between appointments, you can [explore or download Patterns](/get). The point is not to document every thought. It is to make more room for a different response when the loop appears.
