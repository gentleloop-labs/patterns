<p class="lead">
A compulsion in OCD is a repeated action or mental act that a person feels driven to perform to reduce distress, prevent a feared outcome, or make uncertainty feel resolved. It can be visible, like checking a lock, or private, like replaying a conversation. The action may bring brief relief, but the doubt often returns and invites another round.
</p>

This is a description of a pattern, not a way to diagnose a person from one habit. Many ordinary activities involve repetition. What the action is *for*, how it fits with unwanted thoughts, and how much it interferes with life all matter.

## What is a compulsion in OCD, in everyday terms?

An obsession is an unwanted, intrusive thought, image, urge, or doubt that brings distress. A compulsion is what someone does in response to that distress or to prevent something feared. The [NHS description of OCD](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/obsessive-compulsive-disorder-ocd/symptoms/) includes both repeated behaviours and mental acts. Relief after a compulsion is usually temporary, so the cycle can repeat.

For example, a person worries that they sent an offensive message. They reread it several times, ask a friend what it means, then mentally replay the conversation. The first reading may have answered a practical question. The later rounds may be attempts to make doubt disappear. Our [OCD overview](/ocd) explains the broader obsession, distress, response, and relief loop.

The [International OCD Foundation (IOCDF)](https://iocdf.org/about-OCD/) notes that not all repeated behaviour is compulsive. A bedtime routine, religious practice, or careful job-related check can be functional. The same outward action may have a different purpose for someone else, or on a different day.

## The visible rituals people recognize

Some compulsions are easy for another person to notice:

- Repeatedly washing or cleaning to remove a feared contaminant.
- Checking a door, appliance, document, or message again after a reasonable check is complete.
- Repeating a movement, rewriting a line, or arranging an item until it feels “right.”
- Asking the same question in new words after receiving an answer.

These examples appear in the [IOCDF's list of common compulsions](https://iocdf.org/about-OCD/). None is automatically an OCD symptom. Washing after handling raw food is normal hygiene; checking a critical work calculation can be necessary. The question is whether the response becomes driven by distress and difficult to stop even when the practical need has been addressed.

Our guides to [checking OCD](/blog/checking-ocd) and [reassurance seeking](/blog/reassurance-seeking-ocd) examine two common ways a reasonable-looking action can grow into a ritual.

## Mental rituals count too

A person can spend a long time on a compulsion without moving. Mental rituals may include reconstructing an event to prove what happened, silently repeating a phrase to cancel a thought, counting, self-reassuring, or checking feelings for evidence. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/obsessive-compulsive-disorder-ocd/symptoms/) explicitly includes mental acts in its description of compulsions.

An unwanted thought that appears on its own is not the same as a chosen response to that thought. This distinction can be hard to see when both happen in the mind. A [study of 201 people in OCD treatment trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21509914/) found that themes sometimes called “purely obsessional” were associated with mental compulsions and reassurance seeking in those samples. That finding does not imply every intrusive thought is followed by a recognizable ritual.

Our guide to [mental compulsions and “Pure O”](/blog/pure-o-mental-compulsions) gives more examples. If replaying an event is the main response, see [mental review in OCD](/blog/mental-review-ocd). The aim is to name a pattern with a clinician, not monitor every thought until you feel sure which category it belongs to.

## Avoidance and asking others can serve the same loop

Some responses happen before a feared situation: not opening an email, avoiding a road, or delaying a decision until it feels safe. The [IOCDF](https://iocdf.org/about-OCD/) includes avoidance alongside common OCD compulsive responses. It may produce relief without a visible ritual, because the triggering situation never occurs. Our guide to [OCD avoidance](/blog/ocd-avoidance) covers this in more detail.

Other responses recruit another person. A partner is asked to check, confirm, or listen to a confession repeatedly. The question may be sincere and painful; it can still become part of the cycle if each answer brings only short relief. An ordinary request for information is different. No fixed number of questions marks the boundary for everyone.

## How to distinguish a ritual from useful action

Three observations can help someone describe the pattern to a professional:

1. **Purpose:** Is the action answering a practical question or trying to remove a feeling of uncertainty?
2. **Stopping point:** Does it end when enough information arrives, or does the rule for “enough” keep moving?
3. **Afterward:** Is there a useful decision, or only temporary relief followed by another round?

These are conversation prompts, not a self-test. People can have OCD and also face real safety, medical, moral, or legal questions that deserve proportionate action. If the stakes are high, get the relevant professional advice. An OCD-informed clinician can help separate a necessary response from extra rituals rather than asking someone to ignore a genuine problem.

## Why treatment focuses on the response

[Exposure and response prevention (ERP)](/erp) is an established OCD treatment. The [National Institute of Mental Health](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/obsessive-compulsive-disorder-when-unwanted-thoughts-or-repetitive-behaviors-take-over) describes ERP as gradually facing relevant triggers while refraining from the usual compulsive response. A trained clinician plans that work around the person's symptoms and circumstances.

For a checking ritual, response prevention may involve leaving after an ordinary check and allowing the residual doubt to be there. For a mental ritual, it may mean noticing the urge to reconstruct an event and returning to an activity without completing the review. These are examples, not a prescription to skip safety checks or attempt a difficult exposure alone. Our guide to [finding an OCD therapist](/blog/how-to-find-an-ocd-therapist) can help when seeking care.

If you use Patterns, a brief entry about the trigger and response can make the cycle easier to describe over time. Repeatedly editing the record until it feels complete can become another compulsion; [this guide](/blog/when-an-ocd-app-becomes-a-compulsion) explains how to notice that shift. The goal is a useful observation, not perfect certainty about every moment.
