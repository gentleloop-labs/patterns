<p class="lead">
Postpartum OCD is OCD that occurs after a baby is born. A new parent may have recurrent, unwanted thoughts or images and then check, avoid, seek reassurance, or perform mental rituals to feel certain the baby is safe. Having an intrusive thought does not by itself tell anyone what the parent intends; persistent distress and the responses that follow deserve a careful assessment.
</p>

The broader term *perinatal OCD* also includes symptoms during pregnancy. This article describes the postpartum period, though many of the same principles apply before birth. It does not diagnose a parent from the content of a thought.

## What postpartum OCD can look like

A parent may be frightened by an image of accidentally dropping the baby, then check their grip again and again. Another may avoid an ordinary caregiving task, ask their partner for repeated assurance, or mentally replay a routine moment to establish whether the baby was ever at risk. The ritual may briefly reduce distress, but the question returns.

The [International OCD Foundation (IOCDF)](https://iocdf.org/perinatal-ocd/what-is-perinatal-ocd/) describes perinatal OCD as unwanted, recurrent thoughts, images, or impulses followed by attempts to neutralize distress. Those attempts can include visible checking or less visible mental acts. As with other OCD presentations, the impact on daily functioning and caregiving matters.

Many new parents have fleeting intrusive thoughts. A single thought does not establish OCD. The pattern becomes more concerning when the thoughts keep returning, cause marked distress, and are followed by time-consuming rituals or avoidance. Our guide to [intrusive thoughts and OCD](/blog/do-i-have-ocd-or-just-intrusive-thoughts) discusses the distinction more broadly.

## Why the thoughts can be difficult to disclose

A thought involving a baby can feel especially frightening because a parent may fear being judged or misunderstood. They may keep the thought private, seek reassurance anonymously, or describe only the checking while leaving out the obsession. A clinician experienced with perinatal mental health and OCD needs the fuller pattern to assess it accurately.

If you are preparing for an appointment, a short description can help: “I get unwanted images that upset me, then I check repeatedly or avoid caring for the baby alone.” The goal is to describe what is happening, not to prove through exhaustive detail that you are a good parent. Repeatedly asking for certainty can become another [reassurance ritual](/blog/reassurance-seeking-ocd).

The [IOCDF's perinatal overview](https://iocdf.org/perinatal-ocd/what-is-perinatal-ocd/) emphasizes that OCD-related intrusive thoughts and postpartum psychosis are different clinical presentations. Only a qualified professional can assess an individual situation, especially when symptoms are new or changing quickly.

## When to seek urgent care

Postpartum psychosis is a medical emergency. [ACOG advises immediate medical attention](https://www.acog.org/programs/perinatal-mental-health/patient-screening) when it is suspected. Signs can include hallucinations, fixed beliefs that others do not share, severe confusion, or a loss of contact with reality. If a parent may act on a thought, has a plan to harm themselves or the baby, or cannot keep themselves or the baby safe, contact local emergency services or seek emergency medical care now.

Do not use an online article to decide whether a symptom is OCD, psychosis, depression, or something else. If you are uncertain and worried about safety, seek urgent clinical assessment. A professional can ask the questions needed to determine the appropriate response without relying on one symptom alone.

## Treatment can include ERP and coordinated care

[Exposure and response prevention (ERP)](/erp) is a form of cognitive behavioural therapy used for OCD. The [IOCDF's perinatal treatment guide](https://iocdf.org/perinatal-ocd/how-is-perinatal-ocd-treated/) describes ERP as a recommended treatment for perinatal OCD. A clinician helps identify obsessions, compulsions, and avoidance, then plans practice that reduces the rituals while preserving ordinary, safe caregiving.

The treatment plan should be tailored to the parent and the actual situation. It is not a do-it-yourself instruction to take a feared caregiving risk or ignore ordinary safety precautions. If medication is considered, the parent and qualified prescriber can discuss benefits, risks, pregnancy or breastfeeding considerations, and any other health needs. Our guide to [finding an OCD therapist](/blog/how-to-find-an-ocd-therapist) includes questions to ask about ERP training; for this period, ask about perinatal experience as well.

Support from a partner or family member can help with appointments, rest, and ordinary practical care. It can also unintentionally become repeated reassurance or checking on the parent's behalf. If that is happening, a clinician can help the family make a gradual plan. See [how to support someone with OCD](/blog/how-to-support-someone-with-ocd) for that conversation.

## Use tracking as a brief record, not a verdict

If a parent chooses to use Patterns, a short note might capture the trigger and response: “I pictured an accident, asked for reassurance twice, and avoided the routine task.” The app cannot assess infant safety, diagnose postpartum OCD, or replace perinatal care. Repeatedly reviewing entries to prove what a thought meant can pull the journal into the same loop.

The most useful next step for persistent or impairing symptoms is to tell a qualified healthcare professional about both the thoughts and the responses that follow. If there is any immediate safety concern, seek emergency care.
