<p class="lead">
Scrupulosity is OCD that attaches to religion or morality. The obsessions concern sin, blasphemy, having offended God, or having acted unethically; the compulsions look like prayer, confession, checking, and reassurance seeking. It is one of the oldest recorded presentations of OCD, and it is treated with [exposure and response prevention](/blog/what-is-erp-exposure-response-prevention) like any other.
</p>

It is also one of the hardest to recognise from the inside, because the compulsions wear the clothes of virtue.

## What the obsessions look like

**Religious scrupulosity** commonly involves:

- Blasphemous or sacrilegious thoughts and images arriving during prayer or worship, often at the worst possible moment.
- Fear of having committed an unforgivable sin, or of having sinned without noticing.
- Doubt about whether a prayer, a confession, or a ritual observance was performed correctly, sincerely, or with sufficient attention.
- Fear that a thought counts the same as an act.
- Doubt about the sincerity of one's own faith.

**Moral scrupulosity** does not require religious belief and shows up in secular people just as clearly:

- Fear of having lied, even by omission or imprecision.
- Fear of having been unfair, having taken more than one's share, or having benefited unjustly.
- Compulsive honesty: confessing trivial inaccuracies at length.
- Rumination about past behaviour to establish whether it was wrong. Overlaps heavily with [real-event OCD](/blog/real-event-ocd).

## The compulsions

- **Repeated prayer**, often with rules about attention, wording, or a required number.
- **Confession**, repeatedly, in a religious setting or to a partner, friend, or therapist. See [reassurance seeking](/blog/reassurance-seeking-ocd).
- **Seeking rulings.** Asking a religious leader the same question, rephrased, or searching for the answer online.
- **Mental review** of past actions to determine sinfulness. See [mental compulsions](/blog/pure-o-mental-compulsions).
- **Neutralising.** Cancelling a blasphemous thought with a good one, or a specific phrase.
- **Avoidance.** Of places of worship, religious texts, certain words, or situations where an intrusive thought might arrive.
- **Excessive restitution.** Apologising far beyond what the situation calls for; giving away money to correct an imagined wrong.

## Why the compulsions make it worse

The mechanism is the same as every other presentation, with an extra turn of the screw.

Praying again to make the doubt go away supplies relief, which teaches your brain that the doubt was a genuine emergency and that the extra prayer averted it. The next doubt arrives more urgently. Over time the practice that was meant to be meaningful becomes a ritual performed under duress, and many people report that their actual faith is eroded by it - not despite the compulsions but because of them.

Confession has a specific problem: the standard of what needs confessing keeps dropping, because the relief is what is being chased, not the resolution. People end up confessing thoughts, then confessing the possibility of thoughts, then confessing that they may have confessed insincerely.

## Scrupulosity and genuine observance

This is the distinction that matters most, and content will not settle it. Function will.

Genuine practice is usually:

- Voluntary and meaningful, even when demanding.
- Proportionate to what the tradition actually asks.
- Connected to community and to a sense of relationship.
- Something you can complete.

Scrupulosity is usually:

- Driven by anxiety, and performed to make a feeling stop.
- Beyond what the tradition asks - frequently the person's own religious leader is the one to say so.
- Rule-bound, repetitive, and marked by having to start over if the attention lapsed.
- Never quite finishable.

That last point tends to be the giveaway. A prayer offered in faith ends. A prayer offered as a compulsion has to feel right before it counts, and the feeling is the thing that will not come.

## How ERP is adapted here

A clinician trained in OCD will not ask you to violate your beliefs. That is not what the treatment is for and it would be both unethical and ineffective. The target is the compulsion and the intolerance of uncertainty, not the faith.

In practice:

- **Consultation with a religious leader** from your own tradition is common and often decisive. They can establish what the tradition actually requires, which gives treatment a baseline: anything beyond that line is OCD, and you and the clinician now have permission to remove it without touching observance.
- **Response prevention on repetition.** Pray once. Do not restart because your attention wandered. This is often the single hardest exposure and the most productive.
- **Ending compulsive confession.** Agreeing a limit with the clinician, and where relevant with the confessor.
- **Imaginal exposure.** Deliberately holding the possibility - that you may have sinned, that you cannot be certain of forgiveness - without resolving it.
- **Removing avoidance.** Returning to worship, texts, and situations that have been avoided.
- **Uncertainty as the target.** The work is on tolerating not knowing, which for many people turns out to be closer to what their tradition actually asks of them than the certainty they were chasing.

## What this looks like from the inside

I do not have the religious version of this one, but I have the moral version, and I suspect the shape is close.

Mine was honesty. I could not let an imprecision stand. If I told someone I had arrived at seven and it had been seven fifteen, I would need to go back and correct it, and if I did not, it sat there. Not as guilt exactly. More like an unclosed loop with a small alarm attached.

It looked like integrity. People occasionally complimented me on it, which was excruciating, because I knew it was not a virtue - I was not being honest, I was being unable to tolerate the possibility of having been inaccurate. There is a real difference between those and only one of them is a choice.

The exposure was leaving small inaccuracies uncorrected on purpose. It felt genuinely wrong, in a way I had trouble separating from the moral wrongness it was imitating. That is the specific difficulty of this presentation: the OCD is speaking in the register of your conscience, so refusing it feels like becoming a worse person.

You do not become a worse person. As far as I can tell you become a slightly more relaxed one who is now able to be honest because it matters rather than because the alarm is going.
