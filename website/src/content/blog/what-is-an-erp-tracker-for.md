<p class="lead">
An ERP tracker is a place to remember exposure and response prevention practice between therapy sessions: what you had planned, which compulsive response you were working on, what happened, and what you want to discuss next time. It does not need to measure every thought or prove that an exercise “worked.” Its job is to make a useful conversation easier, then let you get back to your day.
</p>

This article explains a way to think about a practice log, not how to design your own treatment. In [exposure and response prevention (ERP)](/erp), the plan should be shaped around your circumstances with an OCD-trained clinician. Patterns is an app we make for recording your own notes; it is not a therapist and cannot interpret them for you.

## What is an ERP tracker for?

Therapy appointments are only part of ERP. The [International OCD Foundation (IOCDF) describes](https://iocdf.org/about-ocd/ocd-treatment-guide/exposure-response-prevention/) planned practice between sessions, after a therapist and client have mapped the OCD pattern and chosen exercises together. The [NHS also describes](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/obsessive-compulsive-disorder-ocd/treatment/) exercises at home between appointments. A short record can help you remember what that practice was like when you meet again.

The useful question is usually broader than “How anxious was I?” For example, did you approach the situation you and your therapist planned? Which checking, reassurance, avoidance, or mental ritual showed up? What did you do when the urge appeared? Was there a barrier you did not anticipate? Those details help distinguish the *exposure* from the *response prevention*, and they give your therapist something concrete to discuss with you.

The IOCDF's [clinician resource](https://iocdf.org/professionals/training-institute/clinician-training-resource-hub/) includes monitoring between-session practice and addressing barriers as treatment skills. A tracker can support that conversation. It cannot tell you whether a particular exercise was appropriate or what to do next.

## A short practice note, not a complete diary

Your therapist may use a particular worksheet or ask for different details. If you are using a simple journal, a note could have four parts:

1. **The plan:** the exercise already agreed with your therapist.
2. **The response:** the compulsion or avoidance you intended to reduce, including mental rituals if relevant.
3. **What happened:** a plain description of what you actually did, without turning it into a pass or fail.
4. **A question:** something to bring to the next session, especially if the plan proved confusing or difficult.

Here is a **fictional illustration**, not an exercise to copy:

> “Our plan was to send an ordinary work email without rereading it beyond the check we agreed on. I sent it, then noticed an urge to reopen the sent folder. I returned to my work, but spent some time mentally replaying the wording. I want to ask whether that mental review was part of my usual checking pattern.”

That note is useful because it includes the planned action, the visible response, and the less visible one. It does not need a minute-by-minute account or proof that the email was safe. If recording the example starts another round of reviewing the email, a shorter note may be enough.

## Why an anxiety score is only one piece of context

Some ERP plans use distress ratings. They can help describe how challenging a situation felt or compare exercises on a hierarchy. The IOCDF's [exposure hierarchy worksheet](https://iocdf.org/resource/building-an-exposure-hierarchy-worksheet/) includes anticipated difficulty as one way to structure practice. If your clinician asks you to record a number, it can be included as context.

A number is not a grade. Distress can rise during a planned exercise, and the most useful detail may be that you stayed with the plan or noticed a ritual that had been easy to miss. A lower rating does not, by itself, show that response prevention happened. A higher rating does not, by itself, mean you went backward. Your therapist can consider the exercise, your response, your functioning, and the wider context together. For more on the limits of ratings, see our guide to the [SUDS scale in OCD](/blog/suds-scale-ocd).

## When to put the tracker down

There is no requirement to record every exposure perfectly. You might notice that you are rewriting a note until it feels exact, checking the same rating repeatedly, or trying to use the app to settle whether you did ERP “correctly.” Tracking can become another certainty-seeking task. Our article on [when an OCD app becomes a compulsion](/blog/when-an-ocd-app-becomes-a-compulsion) explains that design risk.

If the log is taking over, tell your therapist what happens when you try to use it. They can help decide what, if anything, is worth recording for your plan. A missed entry is not a verdict. The point of an ERP tracker is to preserve a few useful observations between appointments, not to become another appointment you have to keep with OCD.
