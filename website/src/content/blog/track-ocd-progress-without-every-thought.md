<p class="lead">
To track OCD progress without tracking every thought, try recording a few meaningful moments: what happened, what you felt driven to do, what you actually did, and what you returned to afterward. A brief weekly look at those moments can reveal patterns that are hard to remember on a difficult day. It is a personal record, not a clinical score or a requirement to document your whole mind.
</p>

Patterns does not diagnose OCD or determine whether treatment is working.

## How to track OCD progress without tracking every thought

Counting intrusive thoughts can be exhausting, and it may leave out the part that matters for a conversation about OCD: what happened next. A record of *responses* might include a check you repeated, a situation you avoided, a moment you asked for reassurance, or a planned action you took despite discomfort. It can also include an ordinary activity you were able to return to.

The [NHS description of OCD](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/obsessive-compulsive-disorder-ocd/symptoms/) explains that compulsions can be visible actions or mental acts, and that relief from them may be temporary. The [International OCD Foundation's ERP guide](https://iocdf.org/about-ocd/ocd-treatment-guide/exposure-response-prevention/) describes treatment that works with triggers, compulsions, avoidance, and practice between sessions. Neither source says you must record every event to get help.

If you are working with a therapist, their plan should guide what is useful to notice. If you are not, a journal can still help you bring concrete examples to an assessment without asking you to decide for yourself whether each event “counts” as OCD.

## A week in three short notes

Here is a **fictional example**, not a tracking target or an exposure plan:

| Moment | Brief note | What it may be useful to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | “Reread an email four times before sending it. Felt briefly settled, then wanted to reopen it.” | Is rereading becoming a repeated check? |
| Wednesday | “Wanted to ask a friend whether the email sounded rude. Went to my planned task instead.” | What helped me continue with the day? |
| Saturday | “Avoided starting another email because I expected the same loop.” | Is avoidance now taking time from something important? |

The three notes show more than a count of thoughts would. They include a repeated response, a different choice, and an obstacle. They do not prove improvement or diagnose the person in the example. The point is to have something specific to reflect on or discuss with a professional.

## A simple weekly review in Patterns

Patterns lets you record OCD events, keep dated journal entries, add an optional distress rating, and view trends over time. One way to use those features is to make a few short entries during the week, then open the journal or trends once for a brief review. You do not need a streak or a complete record for the review to be useful.

Three questions can be enough:

1. **What kept repeating?** Look for a response such as checking, mental review, reassurance seeking, or avoidance across different situations.
2. **What did I do differently, even once?** A small change may be worth noting without turning it into a pass/fail grade.
3. **What do I want to bring to a conversation?** Write down one example or question for a therapist, or for a future assessment.

The entries stay on your device, and Patterns does not require an account. You can [explore or download Patterns](/get) if a private place for this kind of review would help. Its trends reflect only what you entered; they cannot account for missed days, changes in context, or everything that matters in recovery.

## What a number can and cannot show

A distress rating can give context to an event. It is not a grade for how well you handled it. A higher number during a situation you previously avoided might mean something quite different from a higher number during an hour of repeated checking. A lower number is not automatically a sign of progress either.

The [IOCDF's clinician resource](https://iocdf.org/professionals/training-institute/clinician-training-resource-hub/) discusses monitoring between-session practice and considering symptoms alongside functioning and other context. A clinician can help interpret a wider pattern. An app chart cannot do that work on its own.

This is also why missing a day is not failure. If trying to fill the gap would make you reconstruct every thought, you can leave the gap. A rough note may be enough; sometimes no note is the better choice.

## When it is time to close the tracker

Tracking can shift into checking if you keep revising the same entry until it feels exact, reopen the graph to confirm you are improving, or feel unable to move on before every field is complete. Our founder writes more about that risk in [when an OCD app becomes a compulsion](/blog/when-an-ocd-app-becomes-a-compulsion).

If that happens, you do not need to solve it by tracking more carefully. It may be worth telling an OCD-informed clinician exactly how the tool has become part of the loop. The [story of why Patterns was built](/blog/why-i-built-patterns) explains the product's intended role: help capture a useful moment, then let you return to your life.

A weekly review is useful only if it serves you. The aim is to remember a few meaningful examples, not to become the perfect historian of every thought.
